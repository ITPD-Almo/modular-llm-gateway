Executive decision - pursue Option 1, but do not promise an unverified date

We recommend a focused WinWord 2.0, targeting customer shipment by 31 March 1991 [A], with the available ten programmers and four assistants [F p. 12]. This is a conditional decision: Raikes should authorize a release commitment only after a 15 May 1990 [A] feasibility gate. WordPerfect for Windows is expected in February 1991 [F p. 8]; buyers may wait for a second release [F p. 11]. A core-code and process overhaul (Option 2) could add one to two years of delay and uncertainty [F p. 11].

The historical warning is stark: the September 1984 estimate of 365 days became 2,187 actual days [F Exhibit 1, p. 13]. The one-year target is a decision to test, not a reliable forecast.

Requirements: one root cause, one corrective mechanism

Q2 | Why did requirements never settle? (L3b)

Single root-cause diagnosis [I]: Opus lacked an agreed, validated and testable product baseline with a binding change-decision mechanism. The absence of a comprehensive specification when the replacement team arrived [F p. 7], distributed authority among several leads [F p. 5], and subsequent additions were manifestations of that gap.

The timeline is concrete: the вЂњfinalвЂќ specification acquired addenda in October 1987, March 1988 and July 1988; additional features followed a February 1988 Gates meeting [F Exhibit 6, p. 18]. Microsoft had a UI lab, but no documented WinWord-specific user sign-off [F p. 3].

Proposed intervention [A] - validate, baseline, and control changes

Sanderson (marketing) and Slyngstad (program management) should observe representative end users doing common writing/editing/printing tasks, with developers and QA present. Convert observed needs into ranked, testable must-haves with acceptance tests and owners. Raikes approves the baseline at the 15 May gate after engineering estimates. Every later request requires a written impact estimate and a decision about what to remove, defer or fund. This applies elicitation, validation and change-impact analysis (L3b); it does not assume requirements can never change.

Assumption [A]: a focused update is enough for users to complete their essential work. Risk R1 is what breaks if this assumption is false.

Demonstrating the choice without inventing feasibility

Q3 continued | Decision proof and limits

Why Option 1 is preferred: strong first-release reviews [F p. 8] make a targeted improvement plausible, and an announced February 1991 WordPerfect Windows release [F p. 8] makes a one-to-two-year redesign delay commercially costly [F p. 11]. Yet the case provides no verified scope estimate, bottom-up schedule forecast or approved spending cap. Therefore we cannot truthfully assert that Option 1 already clears the threshold. The recommendation is conditional on evidence; claiming certainty would repeat the 1984 estimation error.

Go / no-go logic: proceed with Option 1 only when all four plans are credible and mutually consistent. If they are not, do not call the plan successful. Re-scope and re-estimate; if essential user workflows need major architectural work, reconsider Option 2. Final success is evaluated at shipment against the four binary tests, not at the planning gate.
