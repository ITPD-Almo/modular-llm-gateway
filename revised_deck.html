Case framing

Opus won reviews, but missed the original promise

• Gates wanted a breakthrough word processor in about a year. [F p. 6]

• WinWord 1.0 took more than five years; shipped 30 November 1989. [F p. 1]

• Reviews were strong and sales exceeded forecasts. [F pp. 1, 8]

• 30 March 1990: What should Raikes do next?

MSD | Case 3 | I = fact; I = interpretation; A = proposed decision

Recommendation

Recommend focused WinWord 2.0 - conditionally

• Choose Option 1; target shipment by 31 March 1991 [A].

• Use existing 10 programmers + 4 assistants. [F p. 12]

• Validate must-haves and bottom-up estimates; reserve stabilization time.

• 15 May [A]: approve only if scope, quality, time and cost are credible.

• Avoid a shared-core rewrite in this release; revisit if essential needs demand it.

Decision date and gate are proposed; neither is a case commitment. [F pp. 11-12]

L3a | Expectations

Different stakeholders, different verdicts

• Gates: strong product reception; one-year timing expectation failed. [F pp. 1, 6, 8]

• Raikes / marketing: sales exceeded forecast, but WordPerfect still led. [F pp. 1, 19]

• Developers / QA: pressure and prolonged stabilization; initial expectations inferred. [I; F pp. 7-8]

• End users: positive press is not direct WinWord user acceptance. [I; F p. 8]

• “Best ever” is not a binary, agreed threshold.

Expectations must be assessed stakeholder by stakeholder (L3a).

L3b | Requirements

One root cause behind changing requirements

• Diagnosis [I]: no agreed, validated, testable baseline with binding change control.

• 1986 replacement team found feature papers, not a complete specification. [F p. 7]

• Three addenda followed the “final” specification. [F Exhibit 6, p. 18]

• Observe representative end users; validate tasks and acceptance tests. [A]

• Raikes signs baseline; later requests require impact/trade-off decision. [A]

Microsoft had a UI lab, but no documented WinWord-specific user sign-off. [F p. 3]

Option 1

Why Option 1? The market window is real

• WordPerfect had 45% of PC word processing; Word for DOS 30%. [F p. 19]

• WordPerfect for Windows was expected February 1991. [F p. 8]

• Second releases can unlock buyers; core rewrite may add 1-2 years. [F p. 11]

• But the 1984 one-year estimate took 2,187 actual days. [F Exhibit 1]

• Therefore: prefer speed, but do not mistake target for forecast.

Option 2 may improve long-run sharing, but with additional delay and uncertainty. [F p. 11]

L3a | Success tests

Four binary success tests

• SCOPE: every signed must-have acceptance test passes. [A]

• QUALITY: zero known blockers AND ≥7/8 users complete five tasks unaided. [A]

• TIME: ship to customers on or before 31 March 1991. [A]

• COST: no hires, ≤168 team person-months, within signed spending cap. [A]

• Fail any one → do not claim success.

168 = 14 × 12 effort months; dollar cap unknown until Finance approves it. [A]

15 May [A]

Evidence required before a Go

• SCOPE: user-validated must-haves and no essential core rewrite.

• QUALITY: signed test protocol and stabilization capacity.

• TIME: bottom-up schedule with credible margin before 31 March 1991.

• COST: approved dollar cap, effort ≤168 person-months, no hires.

• If any gate fails: re-scope and re-estimate; reconsider Option 2 if essential.

This gate tests feasibility; it does NOT prove the future release will succeed.

L3c | Risks

Three distinct risks to the threshold

• R1 - Wrong product target (Scope/Time): user task tests; trigger ≥4/8 need core redesign. Boehm #3-4. [A]

• R2 - Stabilization overrun (Quality/Time): early QA; trigger blockers rise 2 weeks. Boehm #2. [A]

• R3 - Key-person loss (Time/Quality): backup module owners; trigger any single-owner critical module after 31 May. Boehm #1. [A]

• R1 changes the choice: if essential needs require a rewrite, reconsider Option 2.
